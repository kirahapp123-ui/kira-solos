THE TWO WATER FOOTPRINTS

Direct use and the water behind electricity

A

On-site water

Water used at the facility—for example, evaporation in cooling towers or water used for humidification. Some facilities use little operational water for cooling, depending on design and weather.

B

Upstream water

Water associated with producing the electricity a facility consumes, plus water used in parts of equipment manufacturing and supply chains. These are often outside the data-centre fence.

System boundary matters: a low on-site water number does not automatically mean a low total water footprint. See the IEA, Energy and AI (2025).

MEASURE WITH CARE

Withdrawal ≠ consumption

WITHDRAWAL
CONSUMPTION

Withdrawal is water taken from a source. Consumption is the portion not returned to the immediate water system, often because it evaporates.

A system may withdraw a large volume and return much of it—or withdraw less and consume a larger share. Context changes the meaning.

A METRIC, NOT THE WHOLE STORY

Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE)

WUE is commonly expressed as annual data-centre water use divided by IT equipment energy, often in litres per kilowatt-hour. Check which water sources and uses are included before comparing sites.

Water use÷IT energy= WUE

A lower WUE may indicate less on-site water per unit of IT energy, but it does not by itself capture local water scarcity, upstream power-plant water, or all environmental impacts. Compare the boundary, climate and energy use too. The Green Grid’s WUI metric (2025) explicitly brings water stress into the discussion.

02 — CONNECT THE SYSTEMS

Water is local.
Impacts are connected.

Environmental effects are not identical everywhere. The right questions are where water comes from, when it is needed, how heat is managed, and how electricity is generated.

ONE PLANET · MANY INTERCONNECTED SYSTEMS
01

Freshwater competition

In water-stressed regions, a facility’s demand can compete with households, farming and ecosystems—especially during dry periods. Risk depends on the source, local basin and withdrawals by all users.

CONTEXT-DEPENDENT RISK
02

Rivers, lakes and aquatic habitat

Withdrawals can alter flows or water levels; discharge from some cooling systems can change receiving-water temperature. Ecological consequences depend on volumes, temperatures, timing, permits and site conditions.

SITE-SPECIFIC EVIDENCE NEEDED
03

Energy and climate pathways

Electricity generation can create greenhouse-gas emissions and upstream water demand. Data centres are only one part of a much larger energy system, and impacts vary with the grid and time of use.

DOCUMENTED GENERAL PATHWAY
04

Polar ice and cold-adapted life

Climate change can affect ice, ocean conditions and cold-adapted species. Any link to a particular data centre is indirect and mediated by its energy and supply chains; attributing a local polar impact requires evidence.

03 — DESIGN FOR THE PLACE

Better cooling.
Smarter choices.

A sustainable design balances water, energy, reliability, local ecology and the full life cycle—not a single metric.

01 / HARDWARE

Direct-to-chip liquid

Moves heat directly from processors into a liquid loop.

BenefitStrong heat transfer for dense servers.Trade-offStill needs heat rejection; leaks, fluid choice and maintenance matter.Best fitHigh-density racks with suitable facility plumbing.
02 / HARDWARE

Immersion cooling

Submerges compatible components in dielectric fluid.

BenefitCan manage high heat density and reduce server fan needs.Trade-offFluid lifecycle, hardware compatibility and service processes.Best fitPurpose-designed deployments with trained operators.
03 / WATER

Closed-loop & reclaimed water

Recirculates coolant and may use treated wastewater as make-up.

BenefitCan reduce demand for potable freshwater.Trade-offClosed loops may still need make-up water; reclaimed water needs quality controls.Best fitWhere treatment infrastructure and supply are reliable.
04 / CLIMATE

Air & dry cooling

Rejects heat to ambient air without relying on evaporative towers.

BenefitCan greatly reduce operational cooling-water demand.Trade-offHot weather can raise electricity use or constrain capacity.Best fitCool climates or designs with enough heat-exchanger area.
05 / ENERGY

Low-water clean electricity

Prioritises cleaner power with a transparent water profile.

BenefitCan reduce operational emissions and some upstream water pressure.Trade-offWater footprints vary by technology and lifecycle; matching rules matter.Best fitLong-term power planning and credible hourly or regional sourcing.
06 / HEAT

Recover useful heat

Captures waste heat for nearby buildings, industry or district systems.

BenefitCan displace other heating energy when a real heat user exists.Trade-offTemperature, distance, season and added equipment affect viability.Best fitSites near compatible year-round heat demand.

BEFORE BUILDING

Put location and transparency first.

Assess basin-level water stress, seasonal availability, sensitive habitats, grid mix, cumulative demand and community needs before siting a facility. Publish comparable water and energy data, explain boundaries, monitor impacts, and invite independent environmental review. Efficient models and right-sized hardware can also reduce unnecessary computation—but demand growth can offset efficiency gains.

04 — WHAT COMES NEXT

The future of
AI cooling.

Some approaches are already deployed; others are evolving or still need stronger independent evaluation. Performance claims should be measured at the facility level.

IN USE

Advanced direct-to-chip loops

Improved cold plates, manifolds and facility liquid loops are being developed for increasingly dense processors. Watch pressure drop, pump energy, coolant quality and the final heat-rejection method.

IN USE

Single-phase & two-phase immersion

Single-phase systems keep the fluid liquid; two-phase systems use boiling and condensation. Both are active engineering approaches, with different fluid, materials, safety, maintenance and lifecycle considerations.

DEVELOPING

AI-assisted cooling optimisation

Control software can tune fans, pumps and setpoints using live sensor data. Savings should be verified against a baseline and checked for reliability, energy-water trade-offs and extreme-weather performance.

SITE-DEPENDENT

Heat reuse & reclaimed-water integration

Projects can connect recovered heat to nearby users and integrate properly treated reclaimed water. Infrastructure, treatment quality, distance and seasonal demand determine whether benefits materialise.

RESEARCH GAP

Comparable water-impact accounting

More open, audited reporting is needed on direct and upstream water, basin stress, hourly energy mix, lifecycle impacts and trade-offs between cooling energy and water consumption.
